How SENTINEL ID Works

Everything in this project, explained from the beginning — what each piece is, what it does, why it was chosen, and what to say when someone asks you something you were not expecting.

Start here: the one-sentence version

A border officer photographs a document; the system checks everything it can actually prove, and hands back a decision plus an honest list of everything it could not check.

That second half is the unusual part. Most systems tell you what they found. This one also tells you what it didn't look at, every single time.

1 · The problem we were given

The SSB guards the India–Nepal and India–Bhutan borders. The India–Nepal border is open — under the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship, Nepali citizens cross without a passport or a visa.

Which means, practically:

  • Nobody knows who is coming. There is no passenger list, no booking, no advance manifest. At an airport the system knows your name hours early. Here it knows nothing until a person is standing there.
  • The documents are wildly varied. An Aadhaar card, a photocopy of one, a Nepali citizenship certificate handwritten by a district office, a voter card, a driving licence, a passport.
  • The same people cross every day. Many are daily commuters — traders, students, workers. A system that wrongly stops someone stops them again tomorrow, and the day after.
Why the obvious approach fails

The tempting build is a neural network that looks at a document and says "94% genuine". It demos beautifully.

But an officer cannot act on 94%. Is that a pass? And if that person is later found with a forged document, what does the officer say in the inquiry — "the computer said 94"? A court cannot review a number. That system fails the only two tests that matter: can a person act on it, and can it be defended afterwards.

2 · The big idea — the trust ladder

This is the heart of the project. If you remember one thing, remember this.

Different kinds of checking produce different qualities of evidence. Checking a cryptographic signature is not the same kind of fact as a model saying an image "looks edited". So instead of mixing them into one score, every check declares which rung it sits on — and a lower rung can never override a higher one.

RUNG 0
Cryptographic proof
The government that issued this document signed it, and the signature checks out. Mathematics, not opinion.
Can clear ✓
RUNG 1
Deterministic arithmetic
Sums that either work or don't. Check digits on the passport strip, expiry dates, fields that must agree.
Can only reject
RUNG 2
The AI
Machine learning. Spotting edited photos, comparing faces, detecting a photo of a photo. Produces a score and an uncertainty.
Can never clear
RUNG 3
Context
"This document crossed four times today." "This number is on a watchlist." Information for the officer.
Decides nothing

Look at the right-hand column. The AI is on rung 2, and rung 2 can only ever push a case toward a human or toward rejection. No matter how confident the model is, it cannot wave anyone through.

The analogy that works on anyone

Think of a hospital. A blood test result is one kind of fact. A junior doctor's hunch that "something looks off" is a different kind. Both are useful. But you would never let the hunch overrule the blood test, and you would never discharge a patient on a hunch alone.

The trust ladder is that rule, written into the software so it cannot be forgotten on a busy day.

How a final decision gets made

There are exactly three possible outcomes, and the rules are short:

  • Any rung-1 failure → REJECTED. The arithmetic didn't work; there is nothing to argue about.
  • Any rung-2 concern → MANUAL_REVIEW. A person must look.
  • A rung-0 valid signature, and nothing above → CLEARED.
  • Anything else → MANUAL_REVIEW. Uncertainty always goes to a human, never to "cleared". We call this failing closed.

One subtlety worth knowing, because it's a real design decision: a concern outranks a valid signature. A genuinely signed document can still have been physically altered after it was issued, so if the tamper detector raises something, the case goes to a person even though the signature is fine.

3 · What happens to one document, step by step

STEP 1
Capture
A photo or PDF arrives from the officer's phone or the console upload form.
STEP 2
Extraction
Straighten the image, find the code strip, read the QR, read the text.
STEP 3
Detectors
13 independent checks run. Each returns evidence — or evidence saying it couldn't run.
STEP 4
The ladder
A pure function turns all that evidence into one verdict.
STEP 5
Record
The case and its audit-log entry are written together, or neither is.
STEP 6
Officer
The console shows the decision and everything unchecked. Any override is added, never substituted.

The rule that makes step 3 unusual

Every detector must return a structured Evidence object. It is never allowed to return just "true", "false", or a number. Every piece of evidence carries: 

detector_id     which check produced this
rung            0, 1, 2 or 3 - how much authority it has
result          PASS / FAIL / PROOF_VALID / INCONCLUSIVE ...
score           only for rung 2, and only with an uncertainty
reasons         plain sentences an officer reads
standard_ref    "ICAO Doc 9303 Part 3 s.4.2.2" - the published rule
runtime_ms, model_version, input_digest

Two of those fields carry the whole philosophy. reasons is written for someone who has never heard of a check digit — there is a test that fails the build if the phrase "check digit" ever appears in one. standard_ref names the published standard the conclusion rests on, which is what makes a rejection defensible in an inquiry years later.

5 · The audit trail — and why it's the best part

Every decision is written into a Merkle transparency log. This sounds intimidating and the idea is simple.

How a Merkle log works, without maths

Take every record and turn it into a short fingerprint. Pair the fingerprints up and fingerprint each pair. Keep going until you have one fingerprint for the entire history, called the root.

Change any single record — even one letter — and the root changes completely. So if you publish the root today and someone edits a record tomorrow, the numbers no longer match, and anyone can see it.

We sign that root with a cryptographic key and write it to a file. That file is a checkpoint. It has to be given to somebody outside the border post — a supervisor, another department — because a checkpoint that never leaves the machine proves nothing: whoever holds the database could just recompute a different history.

A later checkpoint carries a consistency proof: proof that the log only had things added to it, and nothing removed or rewritten. And anyone can check all of this with tools/verify_checkpoint.py — one file that imports nothing from our project.

That is your strongest demo moment. Hand a judge two checkpoint files and that one script, and let them verify your audit log on their own laptop with your system switched off.

6 · Privacy — the rules we cannot break

Aadhaar Act 2016, sections 29 and 37, make storing Aadhaar numbers a criminal matter. So the project has five rules that override everything, including any instruction to change them.

  1. Fail closed. Uncertain always means "a human decides", never "cleared".
  2. Never invent a number. No accuracy figure exists anywhere unless the evaluation harness produced it on a named dataset.
  3. Never store an Aadhaar number. Only a keyed one-way fingerprint (an HMAC) is stored.
  4. Face data is not anonymous. A face embedding can be partially reversed back into a face, so we never call it anonymous. It's encrypted, with a deletion deadline.
  5. Architecture boundary. The brain can't reach the internet or the database. Enforced by a tool, not by trust.

Rules 3 and 4 are enforced by a scan that reads every committed file and fails the build if it finds an Aadhaar-shaped number, or a sentence describing face data as anonymous. That scan caught three of our own sentences.

7 · What it honestly cannot do

Lead with this in your presentation. It is the most credible thing about the project, and it disarms the hardest questions before they're asked.

ThingStateWhy
Screened a real documentNeverEvery figure we have describes documents we generated ourselves.
Clear any document todayNoNo real issuer certificate installed. The mechanism is built; nothing real is in it.
Aadhaar QR signatureStubNo specimen. Guessing the byte layout would make it approve documents it never checked.
Passport chip readingBlockedNeeds hardware we don't have.
Face matching / livenessAbstainsNo model weights, and we deliberately generate no synthetic faces.
Read the second strip lineUnreliableTesseract has no OCR-B training data. It refuses rather than guessing.
AuthenticationNoneNeeds a deployment decision about how staff sign in.
Everything elseWorksLadder, evidence, extraction, storage, audit log, console, reports.
Why this is a strength, not an excuse

In every one of those rows the easy move was to ship something that looks like it works. Instead the system says INCONCLUSIVE or NOT_CHECKED and tells the officer which.

A check that quietly guesses is worse than one that declines, because nobody downstream can tell the difference.

8 · Vocabulary you'll hear

MRZmachine-readable zone
The two lines of <<< letters at the bottom of a passport. Designed to be read by machines.
Check digit
An extra digit calculated from the others. If someone changes a number, the sum stops working. Like the last digit of a credit card.
Verhoeff
The specific check-digit formula Aadhaar uses. It catches every single-digit typo and every swap of two neighbouring digits.
ICAO 9303
The international standard defining passport layout. We cite it by section whenever we reject a document.
Hash
A one-way fingerprint of data. Same input, same fingerprint — but you can't work backwards to the input.
HMAC
A hash that also needs a secret key. Without the key you can't even test guesses. This is how document numbers are stored.
Merkle tree
A structure that produces one fingerprint for an entire history, so any edit anywhere is detectable.
Ed25519
A modern digital signature algorithm. We use it to sign checkpoints.
RFC 6962
The published standard for transparency logs, originally built to police the internet's certificate system. We follow it exactly.
Trust anchor
An issuer's public key that we've deliberately decided to believe. Adding one is a serious act — it's a licence to clear documents.
Fail closed
When unsure, take the safe option. Here, the safe option is always "a human looks at it".
Branch coverage
Not just "was this line run by a test", but "was every yes/no path through it tested". core/ is at 100%.
Property-based testinghypothesis
Instead of testing five examples, state a rule that must always hold and let the computer try thousands of inputs to break it.
ADRarchitecture decision record
A short document recording a decision, the alternatives, and why. We have seven.

10 · If you get a question you can't answer

You will. Everyone does. Here is how to handle it without losing credibility — and honestly, handling it well impresses people more than knowing everything.

The three-step move

  1. Say what you do know about that area — the principle, even if not the detail.
  2. Say plainly where your knowledge stops. "I'd have to check exactly how that's implemented."
  3. Say where the answer lives. "That decision is written up in ADR 0005" or "the reasoning is in the roadmap".

That last step is the one that lands. It shows the project has a memory, not just a demo.

Three sentences that always work

  • "We deliberately didn't build that, and here's why —" (nearly every gap in this project was a choice, and the reason is written down).
  • "I don't want to guess at a number. The only measured figures we have are in the evaluation report, and they're on synthetic data."
  • "That's outside what we could verify, so the system routes it to a human."

Never invent a number under pressure. One fabricated accuracy figure, spotted, costs you more than ten "I don't knows".

The five questions most likely to come

"What's your accuracy?"

Against real documents — unknown, and anyone quoting a figure is inventing it. We have never seen a real document. What we can tell you is the false-alarm rate on our own generated corpus, which is zero, and exactly which of five forgery types the detector catches, which is one.

There's a rule in our repository that a number can't appear anywhere unless our evaluation harness produced it on a named dataset. That rule is why the numbers we do quote can be defended.

"Where's the AI? This looks like rule checking."

The AI is on rung 2 — tamper detection, face matching, liveness — and it's deliberately the least powerful layer. It can escalate a document to a human. It cannot clear one.

That constraint is our actual contribution. A model that can clear a document is a model whose false positives wave forgeries through a border.

"So it doesn't clear anything? What's the point?"

Not today — no real issuer certificate is installed, and we won't fake one.

But here's the thing we found during scoping: of eleven document types in scope, only two carry anything a machine can verify at all, and both are Indian. A Nepali citizenship certificate carries nothing. So on these borders, this system is triage and evidence — not clearance. It tells an officer what was checked, what failed, and what couldn't be checked. That is a large improvement on an unaided glance, and it's an honest description of what's achievable.

"Why should I trust your audit log?"

You shouldn't — that's the point. Here are two checkpoint files and a verification script that imports nothing from our project. Run it on your own laptop. Our system doesn't even need to be switched on.

If it says "PROVED to extend the previous checkpoint", then between those two moments our log had entries added and nothing removed or rewritten — and you established that yourself, not because we told you.

"What would you do with another month?"

Nothing on the model side. The three highest-value things are all outside the code: a real issuer certificate so documents can actually be cleared, a passport chip reader, and OCR-B training data so the machine-readable strip reads reliably.

In the code itself, the remaining item is authentication — and that's blocked on a decision about how a border post signs its staff in, which isn't a technical question.

11 · The one command to remember

If everything else fails on the day, this runs offline in about fifteen seconds:

cd "D:\ps 188"
uv run python tools/demo.py

It screens a genuine document, then a forged one, records an officer override, publishes a chained pair of checkpoints, and has a third party verify them. No network, no server, no browser, nothing to connect.

The full running order, the fallbacks and the numbers you're allowed to say out loud are in docs/demonstration.md.
